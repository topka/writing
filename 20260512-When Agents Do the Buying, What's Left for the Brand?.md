# When Agents Do the Buying, What's Left for the Brand?

## Marketer's insights from a day among UX experts, designers and builders.

I attended DDX Munich conference on Saturday.  
It’s not really a marketing event but it was in Munich and “Innovations and UX” are the two topics exciting enough to get me to spend a Saturday away from my family once in a while.

For an event focused mainly on designers, it nevertheless contained a lot of keywords close to my mind, like brand, user, customer, leadership, etc. Unsurprisingly, the hottest topic is still AI. And the crowd was absolutely great, open and diverse. Designers and leaders in design face and solve familiar kinds of challenges and have familiar kinds of fears: how to adapt to the much faster environment, how to keep teams motivated, not just stressed, how to use new tools efficiently and build new processes on the fly.

An indisputable highlight of the day was the 40-minute live Q\&A by the legendary Don Norman. Just this session alone was worth the ticket price.

But here is one example why it may be useful to attend events on the topics adjacent to your role.

I was intrigued by a talk titled “AI Eats the Journey \- What's Left of Your Brand?”. The talk by Felix van de Sand, the CEO of COBE, was fun and engaging. And it got me thinking…

Felix was building on the projection that soon we will not be doing the actual acquisition/purchase process ourselves, and will delegate that to agents instead. As a result the real brand experience will shift to after the purchase. He suggested designers should focus on designing a “machine-readable brand” in a form of agent-ready APIs. And then they should create loyalty building experiences post-purchase.   
The exciting part was the idea of hyper-personalization as a competitive advantage brands should embrace, moving towards creating segments for each individual user, a “Segment of one”, creating unique individual offers for customers and users. Yes, for established companies with a lot of first-party data and with the current tech, this seems realistic and easier than before. And Felix provided some good examples by brands like Spotify or Starbucks. So…

Do I agree with building loyalty post-purchase? Heck, yes\! Customers are so often left forgotten after they gave their credit card.  
Do I like the hyper-personalization idea? Yes, I do. I have some experience with it and would like to try more someday.  
Do I agree with the point about the brand shift? As a marketer, not fully.

I want to look at the topic more broadly and I can’t ignore it when the “brand” is mentioned.

In my opinion, what should be added to the picture is the mention of brand building that happens before the purchase journey. 

We should not forget that an overwhelming majority of our potential customers are not in buying mode at any given time. Marketers (some) know the golden [95:5 rule](https://minimba.com/b2b-brand-building-95-5-rule/) that says that just a tiny 5% are currently searching for your product. So, how do we address it? We make sure our brand comes to mind (of a customer or of an agent) at the right time. We build the brand, playing the long-term game for the broad market to have more success with the remaining 95% later. How exactly it’s done is specific and not the point now. But it’s also not something a brand can dismiss.

So, in the scope of shifting brand interaction touchpoints and hyper-personalization, I vote for stronger brand before purchase as a prerequisite to success. 

1. If we do not have a way to hand-hold and lead the buyer while they are buying it becomes even more important to have our brand in their mind as a trustworthy and friendly one.  
2. Stronger brand reputation and trust will help to not just convert the customer when they are ready, but will also make them more willing to share their data, which we can then use to set up their own hyper-personalized segment and for building strong brand loyalty.

Thank you Felix, for a thought-provoking talk, and for helping me expand my own views.

Another important (though not new\!) outcome of visiting DDX is that *the collaboration between the functions* in the organization is the key for building a stronger org/product/brand. It was mentioned by many speakers. And Don Norman said something in the end: “*The future is in collaborative intelligence*“.   
I hear it as not only between humans and AI but among humans first of all.

Thank you DDX Munich for the opportunity\! That’s exactly what conferences are for and I got what I wanted.

I think I can add these main outcomes and reminders:

- Designers and leaders in design face and solve familiar kinds of challenges and have familiar kinds of fears: how to adapt to the much faster environment, how to keep teams motivated, not just stressed, how to use new tools efficiently and build new processes on the fly.  
- Embracing uncertainty is a must. You’ll never be 100% sure. 70% is good enough.  
- Finding new creative ways to use our first-party data is crucial and can work both in-product and for marketing.  
- Experiences you create are your moat, not the technology you have (this is something I am still processing, may unpack later).  
- Two-way door framework  
- Disagree and commit  
- “Yes, and” instead of “But”

Here are some thoughts that stuck:

AI

- We don’t let AI make judgements on the summarizations it made

General

- Stay in two-way door framework when you can. Do it fast, come back if needed, iterate.  
- Embrace uncertainty: 70% of information is enough, don’t wait to get to 100%  
- Unlearns: we do not have months or weeks of time as we did before  
- Unlearns: old processes collapse  
- How to show value: use freed time to build prototypes (ideas, vision)  
- You may have a bunch of data (if not new product). Use it\!  
- Technology is not a moat.

Teamwork

- “Yes, and…” instead of “But”  
- Disagree and commit
